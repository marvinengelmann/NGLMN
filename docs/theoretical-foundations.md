# Theoretical Foundations

The theories, models, and ideas that inspired ANIMA's design.

## Affect & Emotion

### Theory of Constructed Emotion

**Lisa Feldman Barrett**

Emotions are not hardwired circuits triggered by the world but are actively constructed by the brain using prior experience, sensory input, and bodily signals. The brain acts as a prediction engine that categorizes affect into discrete emotions through a process of conceptualization.

- Barrett, L. F. (2017). *How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain.* Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. ISBN: 978-0-544-13331-0
- Barrett, L. F. (2017). The theory of constructed emotion: An active inference account of interoception and categorization. *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 12*(1), 1–23. DOI: [10.1093/scan/nsw154](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw154)

### Somatic Marker Hypothesis

**Antonio Damasio**

Emotional processes guide decision-making through bodily states ("somatic markers"). Feelings are the perception of body states, and reasoning without emotion is impaired. The body loop and "as if" body loop both contribute to decision guidance.

- Damasio, A. R. (1994). *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain.* G. P. Putnam's Sons. ISBN: 978-0-399-13894-2
- Damasio, A. R. (1996). The somatic marker hypothesis and the possible functions of the prefrontal cortex. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 351*(1346), 1413–1420. DOI: [10.1098/rstb.1996.0125](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1996.0125)

### Polyvagal Theory

**Stephen W. Porges**

The autonomic nervous system operates through three hierarchical circuits: the ventral vagal (social engagement/safety), sympathetic (mobilization/fight-or-flight), and dorsal vagal (immobilization/shutdown). "Neuroception" detects environmental safety or threat below conscious awareness.

- Porges, S. W. (1995). Orienting in a defensive world: Mammalian modifications of our evolutionary heritage. A Polyvagal Theory. *Psychophysiology, 32*(4), 301–318. DOI: [10.1111/j.1469-8986.1995.tb01213.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1995.tb01213.x)
- Porges, S. W. (2011). *The Polyvagal Theory: Neurophysiological Foundations of Emotions, Attachment, Communication, and Self-Regulation.* W. W. Norton. ISBN: 978-0-393-70700-7

### Emotion Regulation

**James J. Gross**

Emotions can be regulated at multiple points in the generative process: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change (reappraisal), and response modulation (suppression). Reappraisal is generally more adaptive than suppression.

- Gross, J. J. (1998). The emerging field of emotion regulation: An integrative review. *Review of General Psychology, 2*(3), 271–299. DOI: [10.1037/1089-2680.2.3.271](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.3.271)
- Gross, J. J. (2015). Emotion regulation: Current status and future prospects. *Psychological Inquiry, 26*(1), 1–26. DOI: [10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781](https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781)

## Neuromodulation & Neurochemistry

### Neurotransmitter Systems

**Multiple authors**

The major neuromodulatory systems — dopamine (tonic/phasic motivation), serotonin (mood stability), norepinephrine (arousal/alertness), oxytocin (social bonding), cortisol (stress response), endorphins (pain/reward), and GABA (inhibition) — collectively shape affect, cognition, and behavior.

- Berridge, K. C. & Robinson, T. E. (1998). What is the role of dopamine in reward: Hedonic impact, reward learning, or incentive salience? *Brain Research Reviews, 28*(3), 309–369. DOI: [10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00019-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00019-8)
- Cools, R., Nakamura, K., & Daw, N. D. (2011). Serotonin and dopamine: Unifying affective, activational, and decision functions. *Neuropsychopharmacology, 36*(1), 98–113. DOI: [10.1038/npp.2010.121](https://doi.org/10.1038/npp.2010.121)

### HPA Axis & Allostasis

**Peter Sterling, Joseph Eyer & Bruce McEwen**

Allostasis ("stability through change") describes how the body maintains physiological balance through adaptive prediction rather than fixed setpoints. Chronic stress leads to allostatic load — cumulative wear from repeated or prolonged activation of stress mediators.

- Sterling, P. & Eyer, J. (1988). Allostasis: A new paradigm to explain arousal pathology. In S. Fisher & J. Reason (Eds.), *Handbook of Life Stress, Cognition and Health* (pp. 629–649). John Wiley & Sons.
- McEwen, B. S. (1998). Protective and damaging effects of stress mediators. *New England Journal of Medicine, 338*(3), 171–179. DOI: [10.1056/NEJM199801153380307](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199801153380307)
- McEwen, B. S. & Stellar, E. (1993). Stress and the individual: Mechanisms leading to disease. *Archives of Internal Medicine, 153*(18), 2093–2101. DOI: [10.1001/archinte.1993.00410180039004](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.1993.00410180039004)

## Cognition

### Heuristics & Cognitive Biases

**Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky**

Human judgment systematically deviates from rational norms through cognitive heuristics — mental shortcuts that are efficient but prone to predictable errors. Includes confirmation bias, availability heuristic, anchoring, negativity bias, and many others.

- Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. *Science, 185*(4157), 1124–1131. DOI: [10.1126/science.185.4157.1124](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)
- Kahneman, D. (2011). *Thinking, Fast and Slow.* Farrar, Straus and Giroux. ISBN: 978-0-374-27563-1

### Affective Forecasting

**Daniel Gilbert & Timothy Wilson**

People systematically mispredict the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to future events. Key biases include impact bias (overestimating emotional impact), duration bias, focalism, and immune neglect (underestimating psychological adaptation).

- Wilson, T. D. & Gilbert, D. T. (2003). Affective forecasting. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 35*, 345–411. DOI: [10.1016/S0065-2601(03)01006-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(03)01006-2)
- Gilbert, D. T., Pinel, E. C., Wilson, T. D., Blumberg, S. J., & Wheatley, T. P. (1998). Immune neglect: A source of durability bias in affective forecasting. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 75*(3), 617–638. DOI: [10.1037/0022-3514.75.3.617](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.3.617)

### Hebbian Learning

**Donald O. Hebb**

"Neurons that fire together wire together." Synaptic connections strengthen through long-term potentiation (LTP) when neurons are co-activated, and weaken through long-term depression (LTD) when activation is uncorrelated. The basis for associative learning.

- Hebb, D. O. (1949). *The Organization of Behavior: A Neuropsychological Theory.* John Wiley & Sons. ISBN: 978-0-805-84300-2
- Bliss, T. V. P. & Lømo, T. (1973). Long-lasting potentiation of synaptic transmission in the dentate area of the anaesthetized rabbit following stimulation of the perforant path. *Journal of Physiology, 232*(2), 331–356. DOI: [10.1113/jphysiol.1973.sp010273](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1973.sp010273)

### Flow State

**Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi**

Optimal experience ("flow") occurs when challenge and skill are balanced at a high level. Characterized by deep concentration, loss of self-awareness, time distortion, intrinsic motivation, and a sense of effortless control.

- Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience.* Harper & Row. ISBN: 978-0-06-016253-5
- Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1975). *Beyond Boredom and Anxiety: Experiencing Flow in Work and Play.* Jossey-Bass. ISBN: 978-0-875-89261-4

### Metacognition

**John H. Flavell**

Thinking about thinking — the awareness and regulation of one's own cognitive processes. Includes metacognitive knowledge (what you know about cognition), metacognitive experiences (feelings of knowing/not-knowing), and metacognitive regulation (monitoring and control).

- Flavell, J. H. (1979). Metacognition and cognitive monitoring: A new area of cognitive-developmental inquiry. *American Psychologist, 34*(10), 906–911. DOI: [10.1037/0003-066X.34.10.906](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.34.10.906)

## Predictive Processing & Free Energy

### Free Energy Principle

**Karl Friston**

The brain minimizes surprise (free energy) by continuously generating predictions about sensory input and updating internal models when predictions fail. Prediction errors propagate hierarchically — from low-level sensory channels to high-level narrative expectations — weighted by precision (confidence).

- Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: A unified brain theory? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 11*(2), 127–138. DOI: [10.1038/nrn2787](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2787)
- Friston, K. (2009). The free-energy principle: A rough guide to the brain? *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 13*(7), 293–301. DOI: [10.1016/j.tics.2009.04.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2009.04.005)

### Active Inference

**Karl Friston**

Organisms do not just passively update models — they act on the world to fulfill their predictions. Behavior is driven by expected free energy reduction: agents choose actions that minimize the discrepancy between predicted and desired states.

- Friston, K., FitzGerald, T., Rigoli, F., Schwartenbeck, P., & Pezzulo, G. (2017). Active inference: A process theory. *Neural Computation, 29*(1), 1–49. DOI: [10.1162/NECO_a_00912](https://doi.org/10.1162/NECO_a_00912)
- Parr, T., Pezzulo, G., & Friston, K. J. (2022). *Active Inference: The Free Energy Principle in Mind, Brain, and Behavior.* MIT Press. ISBN: 978-0-262-04535-3

### Interoception

**A. D. (Bud) Craig**

The brain's representation of the body's internal state — from visceral organs, temperature, pain, and metabolic condition — forms the foundation of subjective feeling states. Interoceptive accuracy varies between individuals and correlates with emotional awareness.

- Craig, A. D. (2002). How do you feel? Interoception: The sense of the physiological condition of the body. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 3*(8), 655–666. DOI: [10.1038/nrn894](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn894)
- Craig, A. D. (2009). How do you feel — now? The anterior insula and human awareness. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 10*(1), 59–70. DOI: [10.1038/nrn2555](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2555)

## Memory

### Memory Reconsolidation

**Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe & Joseph LeDoux**

Recalled memories become labile and must be reconsolidated — re-stabilized through protein synthesis — each time they are retrieved. This means memories are not fixed recordings but are reconstructed and potentially altered at every recall.

- Nader, K., Schafe, G. E., & LeDoux, J. E. (2000). Fear memories require protein synthesis in the amygdala for reconsolidation after retrieval. *Nature, 406*(6797), 722–726. DOI: [10.1038/35021052](https://doi.org/10.1038/35021052)

### Memory Distortion & False Memories

**Elizabeth F. Loftus**

Memories are not veridical recordings but are susceptible to systematic distortion. The misinformation effect demonstrates that post-event information can alter, supplement, or overwrite original memories. People can be led to "remember" events that never occurred.

- Loftus, E. F. (1979). *Eyewitness Testimony.* Harvard University Press. ISBN: 978-0-674-28777-0
- Loftus, E. F. & Palmer, J. C. (1974). Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 13*(5), 585–589. DOI: [10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3)
- Loftus, E. F. (2005). Planting misinformation in the human mind: A 30-year investigation of the malleability of memory. *Learning & Memory, 12*(4), 361–366. DOI: [10.1101/lm.94705](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.94705)

### Involuntary Memory (Proust Effect)

**Marcel Proust / Cognitive Psychology**

Sensory cues — particularly smell and taste — can trigger vivid, emotionally charged autobiographical memories involuntarily. Named after Proust's famous madeleine episode, this phenomenon reflects the tight coupling between sensory processing, the hippocampus, and the amygdala.

- Proust, M. (1913). *Du côté de chez Swann* [Swann's Way]. Grasset.
- Berntsen, D. (1996). Involuntary autobiographical memories. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 10*(5), 435–454. DOI: [10.1002/(SICI)1099-0720(199610)10:5<435::AID-ACP408>3.0.CO;2-L](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0720(199610)10:5<435::AID-ACP408>3.0.CO;2-L)
- Chu, S. & Downes, J. J. (2000). Odour-evoked autobiographical memories: Psychological investigations of Proustian phenomena. *Chemical Senses, 25*(1), 111–116. DOI: [10.1093/chemse/25.1.111](https://doi.org/10.1093/chemse/25.1.111)

## Perception & Temporal Processing

### Basic Rest-Activity Cycle (BRAC)

**Nathaniel Kleitman**

Biological activity follows an ultradian rhythm of approximately 90 minutes, cycling between phases of higher and lower arousal. This rhythm governs not only sleep stages (REM/NREM) but also waking alertness, attention, and cognitive performance.

- Kleitman, N. (1963). *Sleep and Wakefulness* (Revised ed.). University of Chicago Press.
- Kleitman, N. (1982). Basic rest-activity cycle — 22 years later. *Sleep, 5*(4), 311–317. DOI: [10.1093/sleep/5.4.311](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/5.4.311)

### Subjective Time Perception

**Multiple authors**

Time perception is not a fixed clock but is modulated by attention, emotion, and arousal. Engaged states compress perceived duration; boredom and threat dilate it. Significant events serve as temporal landmarks that structure autobiographical time.

- Eagleman, D. M. (2008). Human time perception and its illusions. *Current Opinion in Neurobiology, 18*(2), 131–136. DOI: [10.1016/j.conb.2008.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2008.06.002)
- Droit-Volet, S. & Meck, W. H. (2007). How emotions colour our perception of time. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 11*(12), 504–513. DOI: [10.1016/j.tics.2007.09.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2007.09.008)

### Habituation & Novelty Detection

**Neuroscience (foundational)**

Repeated exposure to an unchanged stimulus produces progressively weaker neural responses (habituation), while novel or unexpected stimuli evoke orienting responses. This mechanism is fundamental to efficient sensory processing and surprise detection.

- Sokolov, E. N. (1963). *Perception and the Conditioned Reflex.* Pergamon Press.
- Rankin, C. H., et al. (2009). Habituation revisited: An updated and revised description of the behavioral characteristics of habituation. *Neurobiology of Learning and Memory, 92*(2), 135–138. DOI: [10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012)

## Self & Identity

### Cognitive Dissonance

**Leon Festinger**

When a person holds two contradictory cognitions — or when behavior conflicts with beliefs — the resulting psychological discomfort (dissonance) motivates attitude change, behavior change, or rationalization to restore consistency.

- Festinger, L. (1957). *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.* Stanford University Press. ISBN: 978-0-804-70131-0

### Narrative Identity

**Dan P. McAdams & Jerome Bruner**

People construct their identity through internalized, evolving life stories that integrate past, present, and imagined future. Narrative coherence — the degree to which a life story "hangs together" — is linked to psychological well-being.

- McAdams, D. P. (2001). The psychology of life stories. *Review of General Psychology, 5*(2), 100–122. DOI: [10.1037/1089-2680.5.2.100](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.5.2.100)
- Bruner, J. (1990). *Acts of Meaning.* Harvard University Press. ISBN: 978-0-674-00361-0
- McAdams, D. P. & McLean, K. C. (2013). Narrative identity. *Current Directions in Psychological Science, 22*(3), 233–238. DOI: [10.1177/0963721413475622](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721413475622)

### Depersonalization & Derealization

**Clinical Psychiatry**

Dissociative experiences in which one feels detached from one's own mental processes (depersonalization) or from the external world (derealization). Can be triggered by extreme stress, trauma, or sustained fragmentation of self-coherence.

- Sierra, M. & Berrios, G. E. (1998). Depersonalization: Neurobiological perspectives. *Biological Psychiatry, 44*(9), 898–908. DOI: [10.1016/S0006-3223(98)00015-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3223(98)00015-8)
- American Psychiatric Association. (2013). Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder. In *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (5th ed.). DOI: [10.1176/appi.books.9780890425596](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.books.9780890425596)

### Internal Family Systems (Inner Polyphony)

**Richard C. Schwartz**

The mind is composed of relatively discrete sub-personalities ("parts"), each with its own viewpoint and qualities. Parts can be in conflict, and psychological health involves a harmonious, Self-led relationship among them.

- Schwartz, R. C. (1995). *Internal Family Systems Therapy.* Guilford Press. ISBN: 978-1-572-30272-2
- Schwartz, R. C. & Sweezy, M. (2020). *Internal Family Systems Therapy* (2nd ed.). Guilford Press. ISBN: 978-1-462-54146-1

## Relational & Social

### Attachment Theory

**John Bowlby & Mary Ainsworth**

Early relationships with caregivers form internal working models that shape expectations about relationships throughout life. The four attachment styles — secure, anxious-preoccupied, dismissive-avoidant, and fearful-avoidant (disorganized) — reflect patterns of proximity-seeking, separation distress, and reunion behavior.

- Bowlby, J. (1969). *Attachment and Loss, Vol. 1: Attachment.* Basic Books. ISBN: 978-0-465-00543-7
- Bowlby, J. (1973). *Attachment and Loss, Vol. 2: Separation — Anxiety and Anger.* Basic Books. ISBN: 978-0-465-07691-8
- Bowlby, J. (1980). *Attachment and Loss, Vol. 3: Loss — Sadness and Depression.* Basic Books. ISBN: 978-0-465-04238-8
- Ainsworth, M. D. S., Blehar, M. C., Waters, E., & Wall, S. (1978). *Patterns of Attachment: A Psychological Study of the Strange Situation.* Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. ISBN: 978-0-898-59461-7

### Social Baseline Theory

**James A. Coan & David A. Sbarra**

The human brain assumes social proximity as the baseline condition. Being alone is metabolically costly — the brain must work harder to regulate emotion and manage threats without social resources. Co-regulation through social contact reduces the neural cost of coping.

- Coan, J. A. & Sbarra, D. A. (2015). Social baseline theory: The social regulation of human emotion. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 108*(5), 753–779.
- Beckes, L. & Coan, J. A. (2011). Social baseline theory: The role of social proximity in emotion and economy of action. *Social and Personality Psychology Compass, 5*(12), 976–988. DOI: [10.1111/j.1751-9004.2011.00400.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9004.2011.00400.x)

### Theory of Mind

**David Premack, Guy Woodruff & Simon Baron-Cohen**

The ability to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions — to others and to understand that others have perspectives different from one's own. Essential for social cognition, empathy, and predicting others' behavior.

- Premack, D. & Woodruff, G. (1978). Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind? *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 1*(4), 515–526. DOI: [10.1017/S0140525X00076512](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00076512)
- Baron-Cohen, S., Leslie, A. M., & Frith, U. (1985). Does the autistic child have a "theory of mind"? *Cognition, 21*(1), 37–46. DOI: [10.1016/0010-0277(85)90022-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(85)90022-8)
- Baron-Cohen, S. (1995). *Mindblindness: An Essay on Autism and Theory of Mind.* MIT Press. ISBN: 978-0-262-52225-0

## Motivation

### Self-Determination Theory

**Edward L. Deci & Richard M. Ryan**

Intrinsic motivation arises from the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: autonomy (control over one's actions), competence (mastery), and relatedness (connection with others). Contexts that support these needs enhance motivation, performance, and well-being.

- Deci, E. L. & Ryan, R. M. (1985). *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior.* Plenum Press. ISBN: 978-0-306-42022-1
- Ryan, R. M. & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78. DOI: [10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68)

## Sleep & Dreams

### Sleep-Dependent Memory Consolidation

**Multiple authors**

Sleep plays an active role in memory consolidation. NREM sleep stabilizes declarative memories through hippocampal-cortical replay, while REM sleep processes emotional memories and integrates them into existing schemas. Dreams may reflect this offline processing.

- Walker, M. P. & Stickgold, R. (2004). Sleep-dependent learning and memory consolidation. *Neuron, 44*(1), 121–133. DOI: [10.1016/j.neuron.2004.08.031](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2004.08.031)
- Walker, M. P. (2009). The role of sleep in cognition and emotion. *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 1156*(1), 168–197. DOI: [10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04416.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04416.x)
- Walker, M. P. (2017). *Why We Sleep: Unlocking the Power of Sleep and Dreams.* Scribner. ISBN: 978-1-501-14432-3
